Warsaw

Warsaw was the capital of Poland and the undisputed center of Jewish life in Europe during the twentieth century. Before World War II, more than 375,000 Jews lived in Warsaw, making it the largest Jewish city in the world—larger even than New York's Jewish population at the time. The city was a vibrant hub of Yiddish culture, literature, theater, and political movements that shaped Jewish modernity.

Medieval Origins and Development

Jews first settled in Warsaw during the medieval period, but the community remained small until the eighteenth century. Following the partitions of Poland, Warsaw came under Russian rule, and the Jewish population began to grow substantially. By the nineteenth century, Warsaw's Jews had established themselves as merchants, artisans, and professionals, creating a thriving community with schools, synagogues, and cultural institutions.

The Golden Age: 1900–1939

The early twentieth century saw Warsaw's Jewish community reach its cultural and intellectual peak. The city became a center of the Jewish Enlightenment (Haskalah), socialist movements, Bundism, and Zionism. Yiddish writers, poets, and journalists made Warsaw their home. The community supported hundreds of synagogues, schools, theaters, and libraries. Jewish Warsaw was a microcosm of the full spectrum of Jewish modernity: from ultra-Orthodox Hasidic dynasties to radical leftist activists.

The Holocaust: The Warsaw Ghetto

Following the Nazi invasion of Poland in September 1939, Warsaw's Jews faced systematic persecution. In October 1940, the Nazis sealed a ghetto in Warsaw's northern districts, eventually confining over 400,000 Jews behind walls and barbed wire. Conditions were catastrophic: starvation, disease, and cold claimed thousands monthly. Between July and September 1942, Nazi deportations sent over 300,000 Warsaw Jews to Treblinka extermination camp.

The Warsaw Ghetto Uprising

In April 1943, when Nazi liquidation of the ghetto began anew, the remaining 40,000 Jews rose in armed rebellion. The Warsaw Ghetto Uprising became the largest Jewish armed resistance during the Holocaust. For nearly a month, Jewish fighters with smuggled weapons battled the Wehrmacht and SS. Though ultimately crushed, the uprising became a symbol of Jewish defiance and dignity in the face of annihilation. By May 1943, the Warsaw Ghetto was destroyed, and most of its remaining inhabitants were dead.

Legacy

Warsaw's prewar Jewish community was nearly completely annihilated. An estimated 90% of Warsaw's Jews perished during the Holocaust. Today, the city preserves the memory of this lost world through museums, monuments, and the remnants of the ghetto walls that still stand as witnesses to history.
