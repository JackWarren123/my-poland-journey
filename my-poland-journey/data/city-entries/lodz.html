Łódź

Łódź (also spelled Lodz) transformed from a small village into a major industrial center in the nineteenth century, becoming the Manchester of Poland. Its Jewish population grew from almost nothing in 1820 to over 200,000 by 1939, making Łódź the second-largest Jewish city in Poland and one of the most important centers of Jewish industrial life and culture in Europe.

Rapid Growth: 1820–1900

Łódź's explosive growth began after the Napoleonic Wars, when it was incorporated into the Russian Empire. The development of cotton and textile manufacturing attracted workers, merchants, and entrepreneurs. Jews migrated to Łódź to participate in this industrial boom, establishing themselves as merchants, manufacturers, and workers. By 1900, Łódź's Jewish population had reached about 100,000, and the city had become a center of Jewish commerce and industry.

Industrial Dynamism and Social Complexity

Unlike Warsaw or Kraków, Łódź's Jewish character was shaped by industrialization and working-class socialism. The city had wealthy Jewish industrialists who owned textile mills, but also thousands of Jewish workers toiling in those same factories. This economic diversity created unique social tensions and political movements. Łódź became a hotbed of socialist, Bundist, and Jewish labor activism. The city's Jewish community also supported a vibrant cultural life with theaters, publications, and schools.

Interwar Period: 1920–1939

Following Poland's independence in 1918, Łódź remained an important center of Jewish economic and cultural life. However, economic competition between Polish and Jewish merchants intensified, and antisemitic boycotts began targeting Jewish businesses. Despite these challenges, Łódź's Jews maintained their community institutions and cultural traditions. The city was home to important Yiddish publications, schools, and cultural organizations.

The Holocaust: The Łódź Ghetto

In 1940, the Nazis established the Łódź Ghetto, confining over 160,000 Jews in a densely packed area. Under the leadership of the controversial Judenrat chairman Mordechai Chaim Rumkowski, the ghetto functioned as a labor camp producing goods for the Nazi war effort. The ghetto endured longer than Warsaw's—from 1940 to 1944—but at an enormous cost. Starvation, disease, and Nazi deportations killed thousands. By 1944, the ghetto was liquidated, with most of its surviving inhabitants sent to Auschwitz.

Legacy

Łódź's Jewish population was nearly destroyed. The city that once thrived on Jewish enterprise and culture was emptied. Today, efforts to preserve the memory of Łódź's Jews include the Jewish cemetery, memorials, and archives documenting the life and death of a community that made an indelible mark on Polish and Jewish history.
